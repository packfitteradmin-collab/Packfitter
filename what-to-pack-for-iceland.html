Packing Guide — Iceland

What to Pack for Iceland

Last updated: August 2026

Iceland has a reputation for cold, but the thing that actually shapes your packing is change — the weather shifts by the hour in every season, wind drives the rain sideways, and the same day can swing from sun to sleet. The good news is that one packing system handles all of it year-round: moisture-wicking base layers, an insulating mid layer, and a fully windproof, waterproof shell, plus a swimsuit for the hot springs. What changes by month is how much insulation and daylight you plan for. This guide covers the year-round essentials, what shifts season to season, and how to fit it in a carry-on.

Quick Answer

What Should I Pack for Iceland?

Build around a three-part layering system — base layer, mid layer, and a windproof + waterproof shell — that works in every season. Add waterproof footwear, wool socks, warm accessories, and a swimsuit and quick-dry towel for the year-round hot springs. In summer you carry lighter insulation; in winter you add heavier layers, ice traction, and a headlamp. Carry-on is very doable in summer (30–40L) and achievable with discipline in winter (40–46L).

By Season

What Changes Month to Month in Iceland

The layering system stays the same all year — what changes is the amount of insulation, the daylight, and a few season-specific items. Here's the quick version, with month-by-month guides for the exact adjustments and a pre-tuned calculator.

Summer — June, July, August
Mild, roughly 50–59°F, with near-endless daylight (the midnight sun). Pack the full layering system but lighter insulation, add a sleep mask for the bright nights and sunglasses for low-angle sun, and expect rain and wind to still demand the waterproof shell. Peak season, so book accommodation and rentals early. Carry-on-only is very achievable.
Fall — September, October
Cooling into the 40s–50s and getting rainier, with the darkness — and the northern lights — returning by late September. Add a bit more insulation than summer and a warmer hat and gloves. The aurora is the draw; the packing is the summer system plus a layer.
Winter — November through March
Near freezing (roughly 28–41°F) but defined by wind, freeze-thaw ice, and short daylight (as little as ~4–5 hours in December). Add heavier insulation, thermal base-layer bottoms, slip-on ice traction, insulated waterproof boots, and a headlamp. It's full northern-lights season. Carry-on is harder here — wear your bulk on the plane.

Strategy

The Layering System That Works Year-Round

Iceland's weather is wet and windy far more often than it is simply cold, and that's harder to dress for than dry deep-cold. The system that works in every season is three independent layers: a moisture-wicking base (merino or synthetic — never cotton, which stays wet and cold against your skin), an insulating mid layer (a 200-weight fleece or a light packable down jacket), and a windproof, waterproof, hooded shell sized to fit over the mid layer.

The reason layers beat a single heavy coat: Iceland swings between exposed, wind-chilled viewpoints and heated cars, cafes, and pools, sometimes within an hour. A layering system lets you shed and add insulation all day, and it compresses into a fraction of the volume of a bulky winter coat — often the single decision that makes carry-on-only possible. In summer you simply carry less insulation; the shell stays.

That said — if you already own a warm, waterproof winter jacket, bringing it is a perfectly reasonable choice, especially for a winter city trip based in Reykjavik. Wear it on the plane rather than packing it, and if it's warm but not waterproof, pair it with a packable rain shell that fits over it. The layering system is the packing-efficient route, not the only correct one.

    Iceland Packing List

    Base Layers
    2–3 base layer tops (+ 2 bottoms in fall/winter)
    Merino or synthetic only — cotton stays wet against your skin in Iceland's rain. Base-layer bottoms are essential in fall and winter for viewpoint days and aurora watching; summer travelers can carry fewer.
    Mid Layer
    1–2 insulating mid layers (fleece and/or packable down)
    A 200-weight fleece is the daily workhorse; a packable down layer adds warmth for cold evenings at minimal volume. Carry lighter insulation in summer, both in winter.
    Shell
    1 windproof + waterproof hooded shell
    The single most important item, in every season. Fully waterproof and genuinely windproof, seam-sealed, with a hood that cinches — the shell is your entire rain plan. Must fit over the mid layer.
    Packable Rain Shells
    Waterproof and seam-sealed — Icelandic rain arrives wind-driven, not as gentle showers, in every month.

    Everyday Clothing
    3–4 long-sleeve tops + 2–3 pants (1 water-resistant)
    Layering pieces, not standalone outfits. At least one pair of pants should be water-resistant softshell or hiking material; jeans soak through in spray and wind-driven rain and stay wet for hours.
    Wool Socks
    4–6 pairs wool or wool-blend socks
    Wool keeps insulating when damp — and in Iceland, your feet will get damp. Bring one pair more than the math says; wet boots and a heated guesthouse floor make the spare the best few ounces in the bag.
    Footwear + Traction
    1 worn pair of waterproof footwear (+ 1 pair ice traction in winter)
    Waterproof hiking shoes or boots with real tread, worn on the plane — never packed. In fall and winter, add slip-on microspikes or ice cleats: freeze-thaw ice at waterfall viewpoints is the hazard first-timers skip and regret. An optional compact second pair covers dinners and flights.
    Head, Hands & Neck
    Warm hat + gloves + wool buff or scarf
    Wind chill makes exposed skin the limiting factor on how long you can stand at a viewpoint, even in summer. Liner gloves under insulated gloves let you work a camera without going bare-handed in colder months; a buff seals the gap a scarf leaves in real wind.
    Hot Spring Kit
    1 swimsuit + 1 quick-dry towel + small dry bag
    Year-round essential. Lagoons and local pools run all seasons and are best in the cold and dark of winter. Major lagoons rent or include towels, but a quick-dry towel covers municipal pools and budget springs — and the dry bag keeps a wet suit from soaking everything else.
    Light & Power
    Power bank + chargers + EU adapter (headlamp in fall/winter)
    Cold drains batteries 20–30% faster; keep the power bank in an inside pocket. In fall and winter, a headlamp is how you reach trailheads and read hot-spring signs after dark. US travelers need EU (Type C/F) plug adapters.
    Daypack
    Compact daypack (13–20L)
    Lives in the car or on your back: shell, water, snacks, the layer you just shed, and (in winter) traction and headlamp. Water-resistant fabric earns its keep in spray zones.

    Toiletries & Small Stuff
    Standard travel kit + lip balm + moisturizer + sunglasses (+ sleep mask in summer)
    Wind and cold dry skin fast — lip balm and moisturizer are daily-use items. Sunglasses matter in every season; in summer, add a sleep mask for the midnight sun, which keeps the sky bright well past midnight.

    What Changes the Math

    • Bringing a warm-but-not-waterproof coat — Iceland's rain is wind-driven; a genuinely waterproof, windproof shell matters more than raw warmth
    • Packing a heavy winter jacket instead of wearing it — a packed parka consumes 6–10L; worn on the plane it costs nothing

    Common Questions

    Iceland Packing FAQ

    What should I pack for Iceland?

    One system works in every season: a moisture-wicking base layer (never cotton), an insulating mid layer (fleece or light down), and a fully windproof and waterproof hooded shell — plus waterproof footwear, wool socks, a warm hat and gloves, and a swimsuit and quick-dry towel for the year-round hot springs. What changes by season is how much insulation and daylight you plan for; the shell and swimsuit stay in every month.

    Does what to pack for Iceland change by season?

    The core system is the same, but the details shift. Summer (June–August) is mild (~50–59°F) with midnight sun — lighter insulation and a sleep mask. Fall (September–October) is cooler and rainier with aurora returning. Winter (November–March) is near freezing with wind, short daylight, and ice — heavier insulation, thermal base-layer bottoms, slip-on ice traction, and a headlamp. Our month-by-month guides pre-tune the calculator for each.

    Do I need waterproof clothing for Iceland even in summer?

    Yes — a waterproof, windproof shell is the most important item in every season. Icelandic weather changes by the hour, rain often arrives sideways on strong wind, and umbrellas are useless there. If you pack one outer layer, make it a genuinely waterproof, windproof shell rather than a warmer coat that isn't waterproof.

    Do I need a swimsuit for Iceland?

    Yes, in every season. Geothermal pools, the Blue Lagoon, Sky Lagoon, and local hot springs run year-round and are arguably best in the cold and dark of winter. Bring a swimsuit, a quick-dry towel (major lagoons rent or include towels, but municipal pools don't), and a small dry bag for the wet suit afterward.

    Can I do Iceland with just a carry-on?

    In summer, easily — lighter layers pack to 30–40L. Winter is harder because insulation, boots, and traction are bulkier; efficient winter setups land around 40–46L by wearing boots and the shell system on the plane. Compression cubes help year-round. Camera and tripod gear for the aurora is the most common reason to check a bag, and that's a reasonable choice.

    Bottom Line

    • One layering system — base + mid + waterproof, windproof shell — handles Iceland in every season
    • The shell matters more than raw warmth: Icelandic rain is wind-driven, and umbrellas are useless
    • Pack a swimsuit and quick-dry towel year-round — the hot springs are a highlight in every month
    • Summer adds a sleep mask (midnight sun) and lighter insulation; winter adds ice traction, thermal bottoms, and a headlamp
    • No cotton base layers, no jeans as primary pants, no umbrella — Iceland's wet-cold and wind punish all three
    • Wear your footwear and shell system on the plane — that habit alone moves 6–10L out of your bag
